# Creative Direction

Read in stage 3. A direction sheet is a set of decisions, not a mood. It must be executable by
someone who was not in the conversation.

## From brief to keywords

Derive keywords by asking what the brief already decided:

| Brief fact | Direction implication |
|---|---|
| Primary effect is *notify* | Low density, high contrast, information leads |
| Primary effect is *attract* | Key visual leads, information sits in a stable block |
| Audience is *moving* | One idea, seen in one second, large type |
| Audience is *sceptical* | Evidence leads: people, numbers, credentials |
| Carrier is a *lit corridor* | Higher contrast, avoid low-contrast grey-on-grey |
| Carrier is a *phone screen in a feed* | Title must survive a 300 px thumbnail |
| Brand has strict assets | The brand system constrains palette and type |
| Topic is seasonal / emotional | Atmosphere may outrank information density |

3–5 keywords is the right number. Fewer is a brief restatement; more is a mood board.

## Style is four decisions, not one

Separate what is often blurred together:

| Layer | The question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | How is the information organised? | Grid, editorial, centred, diagonal, modular |
| **Image language** | What kind of picture, treated how? | Photograph, flat illustration, 3D, line art, collage, risograph |
| **Colour behaviour** | How does colour act, not which colours? | Monochrome + one accent, full saturation, muted, duotone, gradient |
| **Type voice** | What does the lettering feel like? | Geometric sans, humanist serif, condensed display, hand-drawn, editorial mixed |

A "style" is a specific combination of these four. "Youthful campus poster" is not a style;
"Swiss grid structure + flat illustration + yellow/blue duotone + rounded geometric sans" is.

Name the **main style**, the **supporting style**, and the **components** separately. A main
style with a borrowed component (a riso texture on a Swiss grid) is a decision; a main style
blended halfway into another is noise.

## Palette

Define roles, not just colours:

| Role | Purpose | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Background | The field | Occupies the largest area; sets the temperature |
| Primary | The dominant brand or message colour | Second largest area; carries identity |
| Accent | The one colour that earns attention | 5–15% of the area; used for the CTA, the number, the highlight |
| Ink | Body text | Highest contrast against its actual background |
| Sub-ink | Secondary text | Still passes contrast at the smallest size |

Rules that survive contact with real layouts:

- One accent, not four. Two accents compete and neither wins.
- Check the accent against the background it actually sits on, not against the palette.
- If the client has brand colours, they constrain background and ink, not the accent.
- Never build a palette only from hex values — print, LED, and phone screens shift them.
- Check greyscale: a poster that turns into mud in black and white has no hierarchy.

## Typography

Decide three things and write them down:

1. **Display face** for the headline — its weight, width, and size at final size.
2. **Text face** for body and information.
3. **The size ladder** — the actual pixel sizes at final scale, and the ratio between them.

Practical rules:

- Size the type at final scale, not in the design tool. A 12 pt headline on an A2 poster
  viewed from three metres is a paragraph.
- One display face and one text face is usually enough. A third face needs a reason.
- Match the type voice to the audience: condensed and uppercase reads institutional; rounded
  and open reads friendly; a high-contrast serif reads ceremonial.
- Chinese and Latin faces need to be checked together — the Latin part of a Chinese poster
  is often set in the Latin default, which silently breaks the system.
- **Font licence and embedding are part of the type decision.** A font that cannot be embedded
  or licensed for this use is not an option, however good it looks.

## The key visual

Decide the source before deciding the treatment:

| Source | When | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Supplied asset | The client has a usable photo or illustration | Rights confirmed; check resolution and background |
| Processed asset | The client's asset needs cutout, retouch, extension | The change must not alter the fact |
| Commissioned / generated | No suitable asset exists | Brand marks, packaging text, product structure, and faces are not regenerated from a description |
| Abstract / non-literal | The subject is a mood or a concept | Must still signal the category, or the viewer does not know what it is |

Then decide the treatment: subject scale, crop, angle, lighting, depth, and how it interacts
with the type. A key visual with no relationship to the typography reads as two posters
stacked on top of each other.

## Direction traps

| Trap | Why it fails | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Style chosen before the goal | Answers the wrong question | Goal → audience → structure → style |
| Borrowed wholesale | Carries someone else's composition and licence | Borrow the principle, not the artefact |
| Five directions | Nothing is decided | One direction with one alternate |
| Accent everywhere | Nothing is the accent | One accent, used twice |
| "Make it premium" | Not a design instruction | Name the concrete property: more whitespace, fewer colours, larger type, a photographic treatment |
| Design that only works on the monitor | Fails at the carrier | Test at size and distance before stage 4 closes |
